# The eyes
Biology decides some rules here. The entry point of light into the eyes can be effectively modelled as an attenuating lens. Aften sloshing through a bunch of transparent and translucent layers, light terminates in the retina -- the eye's sensor array. The array is composed mainly of two kinds of cells: rods and cones. While rods are more or less sensitive to just the presence of light (almost ignoring all information which would enable us to perceive color), cones are the ones primarily responsible for 'colored' vision. 

<div style="display:flex; gap:2rem; align-items:flex-start;">

<div style="flex:1;">

Cones have photopigment molecules on their outer surface - similar to how old school polaroid camera's worked by exposing light onto a film. Different cones are sensitive to different to different *wavelengths* incident on the retina. Molecular energy gaps set which photon energies can be absorbed (which you can argue are indifferent to whether you use wavelength or frequency), but it is more affected by wavelength because optical dispersion, biological media, and empirical measurement are wavelength-dependent.
